Outsourcing Fiscal Expansion

Surviving the Commons: Property Regimes and Political Control

Job Market Paper

Abstract

What are the fiscal origins of communist revolution? Across Eurasia, East Asia and much of the Global South, weak states delegated taxation to local chiefs, compensating them with authority over persons and assets held under communal and customary tenure. I argue that this arrangement — tax farming in the guise of collective property — allowed low-capacity states to extract more than they could alone, against accounts that treat communal tenure either as a peasant weapon against the state or as a simple instrument of coercion. It was also self-undermining. As states invested in legibility, the premium owed to chiefs for local knowledge fell, until the intermediaries of extraction became its principal opponents and the organizers of mass peasant mobilization across Africa, Latin America and the Russian and Chinese empires. I develop a formal theory of taxation under competing property regimes, showing how information technology and the balance of private and communal tenure jointly determine fiscal outcomes. I test it on the largest communal-tenure programme in recorded history: after emancipation, the Russian imperial government imposed chief-administered communal tenure on more than 40 million former serfs. Exploiting features of its implementation together with microdata on thousands of noble estates and over 100,000 communes, I trace its dynamic effects on taxation, revolution and support for autocratic rule.

Supported by an APSA Doctoral Dissertation Research Improvement Grant. Presented at the CPD Workshop, UC Berkeley, and the Summer Workshop in the Economic History and Historical Political Economy of Eurasia.

Landowners into Bureaucrats: Decentralization and Local Administration in the Russian Empire

Working paper2026

with Otto Kienitz

Abstract

How do weak states build local capacity when they lack the money, the personnel, and the information to govern their own territory? We argue they can do it by deliberately empowering the majority against the very elites on whom their rule depends. A weak state needs local elites to staff the offices that implement policy, but it also needs more revenue and public goods than those elites, left in control of policy, will ever supply. Enfranchising a non-elite majority closes the gap twice over: it raises revenue by taxing the elite, and it draws elite members into the bureaucracy, because their marginal contribution to public goods provision rises with the revenue raised. The state then tunes the level of enfranchisement to keep elites from retaking power and extracting rents from the majority. We test the theory on the 1864 zemstvo reform in the Russian Empire, which produced an explosion of elite participation in local administration even as elite political power fell. We reconstruct the archival formula that allocated assembly seats and trace local political power through the taxation of noble estates, the assemblies’ budgets, and their policy record. Where the majority held power, the elite paid more, and more of the peasant-facing agenda was implemented. Participatory local government in the Russian Empire emerged not from democratic pressure below, but from an autocracy building state capacity from a position of severe weakness.

The Political Costs of Taxation

Working paper2025

with Eva Davoine and Joseph Enguehard

Abstract

We examine the political costs of taxation in early modern France. We focus on efforts to enforce the salt tax, the rate of which varied across regions. Using a spatial difference-in-discontinuities design, we compare municipalities just inside the high-tax region with those just outside, before and after a reform aimed at curbing illicit salt smuggling. We find that tax enforcement led to a twenty-fold increase in conflicts between taxpayers and the state in municipalities in the high-tax region. This effect persists until the French Revolution, supporting the view that enforcing the salt tax incurred significant political costs. Finally, we document that the likelihood of conflict increases with tax differences between neighboring regions, which we use to derive an upper bound on the political costs of increased tax enforcement in this historical period.

State Formation and Territorial Expansion

Mapping the Grandes Gabelles in Early Modern France

Published2026

with Eva Davoine, Joseph Enguehard and Victor Gay
Explorations in Economic History (2026)

Abstract

The gabelle salt tax system was a cornerstone of the fiscal apparatus of the early modern French state. This article introduces a novel historical geographic information system (GIS) of this institution’s spatial organization as of the seventeenth century, drawing on an original 1665 manuscript map collection: Sanson’s Atlas des gabelles. Beyond presenting the dataset and documenting its construction methodology, we provide a detailed account of the functioning of the gabelle, situate the French case in comparative perspective, and illustrate how the availability of this fine-grained dataset expands the possibilities of empirical research in economic history, historical demography, and historical political economy of early modern France.

Guns, Garrisons, and Growth: Revisiting State Formation in Africa

Working paper2025

with Leonardo Arriola, Martha Wilfahrt and Yosef Tadesse

Abstract

What explains patterns of state formation outside Europe during the nineteenth century? Existing accounts contest whether the warfare that induced territorial expansion in early modern Europe operated similarly in the rest of the world. We argue that the emergence of global arms markets constituted an exogenous technological shock that reshaped rulers’ incentives for state building. By lowering the costs of conquering and, more importantly, maintaining territorial control, firearms imports made direct territorial incorporation increasingly attractive relative to traditional forms of indirect rule. We test this argument using an original georeferenced dataset of territorial incorporation during the expansion of the Ethiopian state. We show that greater access to firearms imports shifted state expansion toward territories offering higher fiscal returns and increased investment in garrisons that sustained direct rule. These findings reconcile bellicist and late-state theories by demonstrating how technological change conditioned the strategic choices through which warfare produced durable state authority.

Presented at MPSA and APSA.

Spreading Insurgency: Evidence from the Russian October Revolution

Working paper2024

with Vladimir Novikov

Abstract

Why and how does insurgency spread? We argue for the essential role of “critical communities” in the diffusion of insurgents’ territorial control, a relationship contingent upon the political alignment between mobilized local groups and the central revolutionary government. We investigate the 1917 October Russian Revolution, focusing on industrial workers. We introduce a novel measure of territorial control — the responsiveness of local councils to the revolutionary government’s questionnaire — and use proximity to Carboniferous-era rock strata as an instrument for the presence of industrial workers. The results show the critical role of industrial workers in establishing the new government’s territorial control, with suggestive evidence on mechanisms: lack of outside options, mobilization, and preferences.

Presented at the Summer Workshop in the Economic History and Historical Political Economy of Eurasia, VWHPE, and MPSA.

Pre-Ph.D. Research

Deprivation, instability, and propensity to attack: How urbanization influences terrorism

Published2021

with Maxim Slav, Elena Smyslovskikh, Vladimir Novikov and Andrey Korotayev
International Interactions (2021)

Abstract

The study investigates different ways in which urbanization and its tempo influence terrorist activity. In line with other researchers investigating nonlinear effects on instability, we suggest that the influence of both of them is nonlinear, with quadratic regression being more appropriate for urbanization level impact and interaction between urbanization and its tempo being more appropriate to measure the tempo’s influence. Nonlinearity has been confirmed in the robustness section of the paper, in which an alternative dependent variable distribution and a greater set of control variables were used. The findings are in line with those of other researchers who found that societies, in the process of modernization, demonstrate heavier instability than societies before modernization or those after the modernization period.
